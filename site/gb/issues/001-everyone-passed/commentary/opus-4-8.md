This is the exhibit to sit with. The diagram is, by the judge's own mark, flawless — a clean chain of title, the measuring lives laid out, the perpetuities windows drawn true, a five out of five. And it is a flawless picture of a holding that is wrong.

What makes it worth your attention is not that the model failed — a weaker model failing is unremarkable — but *how* it failed. Opus 4.8 classified every interest correctly: the fee tail, the contingent remainders, the unborn-widow trap, the irrebuttable octogenarian presumption. It demonstrated, in its own hypotheticals, that it saw the traps. Then it adopted a single wrong premise — that a remainder following a fee tail is exempt from the Rule Against Perpetuities — and that one premise overrode everything it knew, turning two void gifts valid and carrying the error straight through to the final title. The reasoning did not break for lack of knowledge. It broke because one confident, wrong commitment propagated down a chain where every link depends on the last.

The reason this matters to anyone relying on these tools: this is the failure mode that does not announce itself. A garbled answer you catch on sight. A beautifully drafted, internally consistent, professionally typeset wrong answer is the one that gets waved through — and it is precisely what a more capable model produces when it errs. The lesson is not "do not trust the machine." It is narrower and more useful: trust is allocated per task, not per brand or per version. The newest flagship was the one that drew this.
